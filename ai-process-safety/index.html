Using AI to Support Safer Maintenance and Turnarounds

What if a maintenance team could check the equipment, surrounding work, and available hazard information together before a job begins?
By Nikhil Sunku   /   Industrial technology

A mechanic arrives to replace a pump and a short section of its connected piping. A nearly identical pump sits a few feet away. A scaffold is being assembled nearby, another crew is preparing for hot work, and the job may require entry into a below-grade space. Before the first connection is opened, the team needs to be certain which equipment is in scope and which hazards are present.

This is where I see a useful role for AI. An application could bring a work order, equipment identifiers, current drawings, permits, and relevant sensor information into one view. It could point out a mismatch or missing information that deserves attention. It could also help teams see when several jobs may interfere with one another during a turnaround.

The purpose is to give people another opportunity to catch a problem. A camera cannot prove that a line is empty. A model cannot authorize entry. Operations, maintenance, safety professionals, and the permit system remain responsible for the decisions and checks that make the work safe.

The idea in one sentence

Use AI to make it easier for the right people to see the right information, ask better questions, and stop when something does not agree.

Start with a real work problem

Imagine a planned replacement of pump P-204A and one section of pipe connected to it. Pump P-204B is next to it and looks similar. In this hypothetical example, part of the work is inside a below-grade vault that the site has assessed as a permit-required confined space.

Replacing a pump does not automatically require confined-space entry. The site must assess the actual space, the work, and its hazards. A job can also involve several kinds of risk at once: pressure or residual chemicals in a line, unexpected electrical or mechanical energy, a hazardous atmosphere, suspected asbestos-containing insulation, or work taking place above the crew.

If a mechanic is uncomfortable because a drawing does not match the field, a label is missing, or a vent is in an unexpected location, that concern deserves a pause and a conversation. A good system should make it easier to raise that concern, without treating the worker as the problem.

Use the camera to check the equipment

Before the permit is issued, or during the site walkdown, an authorized worker could use a site-approved mobile device to capture a photo or use a live video view of the equipment tag and the surrounding area. Optical character recognition (OCR) could read the visible tag, while computer vision could help find the tag and suggest equipment or nearby features. The application could compare what it sees with the work order and the approved asset register.

Example of an advisory prompt

Work order: P-204A, north side of the unit
Tag read from image: P-204B
Application: “The tag appears to differ from the work order. Stop and verify the asset with operations before continuing.”

If the view is blurred, the tag is damaged, the location is uncertain, or records disagree, the application should say “unable to verify” and ask for a human review. It should show what was read and where the supporting information came from, not present a green “safe to work” message. Even an apparent match can be wrong: a camera can misread a character, and physical tags or records can be incorrect. The same approach could compare an entry-point label with the location on a permit, while the crew independently verifies the actual space and work location.

The pump tag alone is not enough to identify a pipe or prove its contents. The crew must identify the correct line and connection using site procedures, current approved piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs), physical tracing, and verification with the responsible operations personnel. A photo or QR code is an aid to that work, not proof that the right line is isolated or depressurized.

Connect the plant map and sensor information

A useful application needs context beyond an image. An approved plant map could associate equipment tags, line numbers, vessels, access points, and work areas with the current asset register. It could also link to applicable drawings, maintenance history, hazard records, and active permits. The map needs an owner and a way to reflect approved changes; an old drawing or an unrecorded field change can make a convincing answer wrong.

Sensor fusion means combining information from more than one source to give the team a fuller picture. For this job, that might include:

  • Equipment identity: the work order, physical tag, line identification, and approved map.
  • Worksite context: nearby open permits, scaffold work, lifting activity, and the location of vents or drains recorded by the site.
  • Available process information: selected pressure, temperature, or operating-state data from an approved source, with its timestamp and quality status.
  • Hazard records: documented space classifications, chemical information, and known or suspected asbestos locations.

The display should make the source, age, and quality of each item visible. Missing, stale, conflicting, or out-of-service data should be clearly marked. Indoor location estimates can be inaccurate, and a sensor value can be wrong or represent a different part of the process. An AI summary must not turn those uncertainties into an assurance.

For example, a pressure reading on a dashboard does not establish that the particular section to be opened has been isolated, drained, or verified. The crew must follow the site’s line-opening and energy-control procedures, including physical verification by authorized personnel.

Confined spaces, asbestos, and other hazards

Confined-space entry

If the site determines that entry is permit-required, the existing entry program governs the job. In U.S. general industry, the applicable requirements are set out in OSHA’s permit-required confined-space standard. Depending on the space and the applicable procedures, the team must address isolation, atmospheric testing and monitoring, ventilation, an attendant, communications, rescue arrangements, and authorization by the entry supervisor.

AI could remind the team that the selected location has a confined-space record or highlight an expired or missing document. It cannot measure the atmosphere through a camera, replace calibrated testing, or decide that the space is safe. Even where a site’s program uses a continuous monitor, its alarms and required response must operate independently of the AI application. A nearby nitrogen source, vent, or changing process condition may affect the atmosphere; actual conditions must be assessed at the worksite.

Suspected asbestos and insulation

Older insulation, gaskets, or other materials may require an asbestos assessment. The application could flag a known or suspected location from the site’s records before a pipe or pump is disturbed. A photo cannot establish that material does or does not contain asbestos. If material is suspect or the records are unclear, workers should stop disturbing it and involve the site’s qualified asbestos or industrial hygiene personnel under the applicable procedures.

Scaffolding and work above the crew

A scaffold may be needed for access to a pump, valve, or line. The application could show a nearby scaffold job or flag that a lift is planned above another crew. People still need to inspect the work area, follow scaffold and fall-protection requirements, manage dropped-object exposure, and coordinate access. The appropriate requirements depend on the work and jurisdiction.

Help people prepare permits, not approve them

Before a permit is opened or issued, the application could assemble a draft of relevant information: the work order, equipment tags, planned work area, known hazards, linked procedures, and nearby work. It could flag a missing document, an unexpected drawing revision, or a possible conflict for the permit issuer and work crew to investigate.

For the pump example, the team might review whether the correct line-opening, isolation, and confined-space processes apply. Operations and authorized employees carry out the required isolations, control stored energy, and verify the equipment under the site’s procedures. OSHA’s hazardous-energy standard establishes requirements for this control where it applies. The entry supervisor makes the applicable entry authorization. AI does not sign the permit, clear a hazard, direct an isolation, or replace the field walkdown.

If the application is unavailable, the site’s independent permit and safety processes still apply. If it identifies a mismatch, workers should stop the affected activity and resolve it through those processes. A phone screen is never a reason to bypass a stop-work concern.

Make turnaround conflicts easier to see

During a turnaround, many crews and contractors work in the same unit over a short period. A pipe replacement may sit next to welding, a crane lift, scaffold assembly, insulation removal, or testing and startup work. Each permit may look reasonable by itself while the combination creates a problem.

A planning tool could compare job locations, work windows, and descriptions to flag possible simultaneous-operations conflicts. For example, it might show a proposed hot-work job near a line-opening task, or a lift scheduled above a confined-space entry point. A coordinator would check the actual locations, timing, barriers, and current conditions with the affected crews, then decide how to sequence or control the work.

The plan can change during a shift. Delays, scope changes, newly discovered hazards, and handovers should trigger review under site procedures. An automatically generated schedule is only as good as the updates it receives; it does not replace coordination in the field.

Look for recurring warnings, then investigate

The same issue appearing repeatedly can be a useful signal. If a team sees several equipment-tag mismatches in one area, repeated references to an outdated drawing, or the same isolation question across multiple work orders, the application could group those reports for a safety or reliability review.

For example, three reports of a tag mismatch over a month might lead the team to inspect the physical labels, check the asset register, and review how work orders are prepared. The cause could be a mislabeled asset, confusing instructions, a data problem, or an OCR error. The count alone does not establish a root cause. Someone needs to investigate, assign an owner, document corrective action, and check whether the issue was actually resolved.

Near misses and worker observations are especially valuable when people can report them without fear of blame. Use the information to improve the work and the system, with access controls and a clear purpose, not to score or monitor individuals.

Keep the system within clear boundaries

These tools would handle sensitive plant information and would operate near equipment where mistakes have consequences. A practical design should include the following limits:

  • Advisory operation: no automatic permit approval, valve movement, equipment start or stop, or changes to safety systems.
  • Approved data paths: selected information can come from site-approved read-only copies, such as a historian replica, through an appropriate network boundary. OT systems do not need direct internet exposure. OT, engineering, and security teams should review any connection.
  • Suitable devices: cameras and mobile devices must be approved for the work area, including the applicable hazardous-location classification. The application must not encourage taking a device into an area where it is not permitted.
  • Controlled information: restrict access to drawings, images, permits, and location data. Follow site rules for photography, retention, contractors, and sharing.
  • Independent safeguards: existing alarms, protective functions, permits, and emergency procedures continue to work without the AI application. A model failure or network outage must not defeat them.

Start with a small, measurable pilot

I would begin with one equipment area and one task: identifying the correct pump and presenting the associated work-order information during a walkdown. Use the application in advisory mode alongside the normal process, with operations, maintenance, safety, and contractor representatives involved in its design and review.

Before relying on it, test photographs of similar adjacent equipment, damaged or obscured labels, poor lighting, misleading angles, and deliberately mismatched work orders. Also test stale records, missing sensor readings, bad location estimates, and loss of network service. Do not create unsafe plant conditions to test the system.

Record how often the application identifies a prepared mismatch, misses one, or raises an unnecessary alert. Review whether the explanations are understandable and whether the extra step creates confusion or alert fatigue. Compare the results with a baseline and check field observations with the people doing the work. Expand only after the responsible teams resolve the gaps and agree that the tool is useful.

The aim is fewer opportunities for a mistake to go unnoticed and better information for the people making decisions. Safer planning may also help avoid equipment damage and unplanned outages, but a reduction in injuries, failures, or downtime should be claimed only when actual evidence supports it.

Good maintenance depends on people knowing the equipment, questioning what does not look right, and working together. AI can support that judgment by making overlooked information easier to find. It should never make someone feel that a screen knows the plant better than the people responsible for it.
